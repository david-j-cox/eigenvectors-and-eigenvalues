Preliminary report · 11 September 2026

Individual transition operators in a signalled two-alternative task

Thirteen participants, 55,380 responses. Every fitted operator predicts held-out behavior better than persistence. Whether those operators track the arranged contingency rather than the signal that announces it is supported but not firmly established.

Abstract

A behavioral dynamics account treats an individual's responding as a state that evolves under a transition operator, whose eigenstructure characterizes that individual. Testing this prospectively requires three things the usual group designs do not deliver: enough transitions per person to estimate an operator, state coordinates that carry signal rather than sampling noise, and the same environment presented more than once so an operator can be checked against itself.

Thirteen participants each emitted 4,260 responses in a two-alternative task with depleting reinforcement patches under three signalled contexts, arranged as an ABAB reversal with embedded perturbations. Operators were fitted per participant per context by ridge regression on ten-response state bins and evaluated on temporally held-out data.

Operators were recoverable for all thirteen participants, and every one predicted held-out behavior better than a persistence baseline (median skill 0.489, p = 0.0002). An operator fitted to the earlier exposure of a context predicted the later exposure in 92% of 52 comparisons (p = 1.6 × 10-7). Ten of thirteen participants showed operators more similar across contexts sharing a contingency than across contexts sharing a color (p = 0.040 Wilcoxon, p = 0.092 sign test). Dominant-eigenvector similarity between repeated exposures was 0.593, below the 0.735 expected from two estimates of the same operator at this number of transitions, so that measure is uninformative here and the replication claim rests on held-out prediction instead.

1Introduction

Behavior analysis treats the individual as the unit of analysis, yet the quantities usually reported — matching parameters, discounting rates, response rates — are static descriptions. They say what an organism does at equilibrium, not how it moves between states. A dynamical description would instead ask what operator carries this organism's state from one moment to the next, and whether that operator is a stable property of the individual.

An earlier study in this program collected 55,454 responses from 60 participants in a dynamic foraging task and showed, on reanalysis, that per-participant transition operators could be estimated and that their dominant eigenvectors differed between individuals. That analysis was retrospective. The operator was fitted to data collected for another purpose, and nothing in the design allowed an operator to be tested against a second presentation of the same environment.

This study is the prospective version. Three questions follow from the reanalysis and organize what follows. First, can an operator be estimated per individual at all, in the sense of predicting that individual's later behavior better than assuming they continue as they were? Second, does an operator estimated in one exposure to a context hold when the same context returns? Third, when a context changes, do the dynamics follow the arranged contingency or the perceptual signal that announces it — that is, is the operator a property of the organism's relation to reinforcement or of the stimulus?

2Method

2.1 Participants

Thirteen adults recruited through Prolific, paid $6.67 for a session estimated at 40 minutes ($10.00/hour). Participants who had taken part in earlier studies in this program, or in the three pilot studies that developed this instrument, were excluded at recruitment. Sessions ran 17.7 to 32.4 minutes of responding (mean 22.5). All thirteen completed the full 4,260 responses.

2.2 Apparatus and task

A browser task presenting two identical response panels against a full-viewport background. Responses were made by clicking a panel or pressing F or J. A 150 ms cooldown discarded responses arriving faster than that. The background color, paired with a texture so the discrimination did not rest on hue, signalled which reinforcement contingency was in force.

2.3 Reinforcement schedule

Each alternative was a depleting patch with a latent value in [0, 1] read directly as the probability that a response produced a point. Working an alternative depleted it by a fixed amount per response; both alternatives recovered by a fixed amount per response made anywhere in the session.

Recovery is counted in responses, not seconds. An earlier version recovered per unit time while depleting per response, which made the schedule a participant met depend on how fast they happened to respond: across six pilot participants, median inter-response time correlated with mean patch richness at r = 0.992 and with obtained reinforcement rate at r = 0.982. Both quantities are now in the same currency, and holding allocation fixed while varying response rate from 150 to 700 ms leaves richness unchanged to within 10-9.

2.4 Design

Blocks were defined by response count rather than elapsed time, because every downstream estimate is a per-response state transition and a time-defined block yields an unpredictable number of them.

practice
60 responses, neutral gray
stages 1–4
8 × 100 responses each, colors alternating strictly
perturbation
5 × 200 responses, 8 perturbations (4 extinction, 4 contingency reversal)

The four stages form an ABAB reversal: stage 3 restores the color-to-contingency mapping of stage 1 and stage 4 that of stage 2. This yields, for every participant, two exposures to each color × contingency cell that are matched on elapsed time, which is what makes a same-color comparison and a same-contingency comparison interpretable against each other. The mapping from color to contingency was randomized per participant.

2.5 Analysis

Responses were aggregated into non-overlapping ten-response bins within context. Each bin contributed a state vector of choice proportion, obtained reinforcement rate and mean log inter-click interval. Operators were fitted per participant per context by ridge regression predicting the next state from the current one, with the regularization parameter chosen on an inner temporal split of the training data; the earliest exposures were used for training and the latest for testing. Skill is the proportional reduction in held-out mean squared error relative to a persistence baseline that predicts the next state equals the current one.

Switch rate was excluded from the state; section 3.4 gives the reason. Similarity between two operators is the absolute cosine between their dominant eigenvectors, interpreted against a floor obtained by simulating two independent estimates of the same operator at the same number of transitions.

3Results

3.1 Allocation

All thirteen participants allocated near indifference overall (median P(A) = 0.50, range 0.44–0.57) while moving substantially within and across contexts. That movement is the signal the operator is fitted to.

3.2 Operators are recoverable for every participant

Operators were fitted for 13 of 13 participants, and every one predicted held-out behavior better than persistence (median skill 0.489, interquartile range [0.423, 0.540]; sign test and Wilcoxon both p = 0.0002). Spectral radii ranged 0.12 to 0.78, so all operators were contractive, and five of thirteen had a complex dominant pair, indicating oscillatory rather than purely decaying return to a fixed point.

The dominant coordinate differed between participants — five loaded principally on choice proportion, four on reinforcement rate and four on switch rate — so the modes are not all the same shape. That heterogeneity is the premise the program is testing rather than a result in itself, since nothing here establishes that a given participant's mode is stable enough to be called theirs.

3.3 An operator predicts the later exposure of its own context

An operator fitted to the earlier exposure of a color × contingency cell predicted the later exposure with positive skill in 48 of 52 comparisons (92%; median skill 0.503, Wilcoxon p = 1.6 × 10-7).

The eigenvector similarity measure is uninformative at this sample size. Median dominant-eigenvector similarity between the two exposures was 0.593. Two independent estimates of the same operator at 36 transitions have a median similarity of 0.735, with a 5th–95th percentile range of [0.087, 0.982]. The observed value lies below that median and well inside that range, so it distinguishes nothing.

This is a property of the estimator at 36 transitions per cell, not evidence against replication. The held-out prediction result above tests the same idea with far more power, and is what the replication claim should rest on until the design provides more transitions per cell.

3.4 Switch rate carries no usable variation for most participants

Switch rate can always be counted. The question is whether it varies between bins by more than the binomial noise of counting it, which the noise ratio expresses as sampling variance over total between-bin variance. Five of thirteen participants fell below the 0.6 criterion; for the other eight, the coordinate contributed no variation an operator could use. Median switch rate was 0.077 (range 0.042–0.200), low enough that ten-response bins contain mostly zero or one switch.

Comparing candidate state spaces by their ability to discriminate between participants, choice proportion with reinforcement rate and log inter-click interval reached AUC 0.832 at 80 transitions, against 0.810 for the state containing switch rate, 0.795 for all four coordinates and 0.792 for choice with reinforcement rate alone. Switch rate was therefore dropped.

3.5 Contingency over color

For ten of thirteen participants, operators were more similar across contexts that shared a contingency but differed in color than across contexts that shared a color but differed in contingency (median difference +0.069; Wilcoxon p = 0.040, sign test p = 0.092). Across all 52 comparisons the medians were 0.746 and 0.603.

Both arms sit below the same-operator floor discussed in section 3.3, so neither absolute value should be read as an estimate of true similarity. The contrast between them is nonetheless interpretable, because the floor applies equally to both arms and the comparison is within participant.

4Discussion

Operators are estimable per individual in this task, and they predict held-out behavior for every participant tested. That is the precondition the program needed and it is met without qualification. The second question — whether an operator holds when its context returns — is answered affirmatively by held-out prediction and not at all by eigenvector similarity, which at 36 transitions per cell cannot distinguish a replicating operator from a non-replicating one. Reporting only the similarity figure would have understated the evidence; reporting it as a positive result, as an earlier draft of this analysis did, would have overstated it.

The third question has a positive but not decisive answer. Ten of thirteen participants show dynamics that track the contingency rather than the signal, which is the direction the account predicts, at p = 0.040 by one test and p = 0.092 by a more conservative one. With thirteen participants that is where the evidence sits.

4.1 Limitations

Thirteen participants is a small sample for the between-participant claim in section 3.5, and the sign test does not reach conventional significance. The eigenvector similarity measure needs more transitions per cell than this design provides; extending the reversal stages by 50% would supply roughly 1,200 responses per cell and raise the usable bin ceiling from 20 to 30, at a cost of about eight minutes per session.

The schedule is richer than intended. Participants obtained roughly 4.1 reinforcers per ten-response bin against a design floor of 2.0, and switching fell as the schedule was corrected — median switch rate was 0.082 under the original changeover delay, 0.086 after it was shortened, and 0.058 once the rate-dependence was removed. A plausible reading is that staying became more profitable than leaving. Whether the low switch rates reported in section 3.4 are a property of participants or of a schedule that pays too well for exploration is not separable here.

Perturbation responses are included in the session records of Figure 1 but are not analyzed in this report. Each perturbation lasts 8 responses against a 10-response state bin, so no bin is purely within a perturbation; recovery after offset is measured cleanly but the perturbation itself is not.

4.2 Data provenance

Three of the thirteen participants were collected in a final pilot that ran the same instrument; a replay of their own response sequences through the current build produces identical reinforcement outcomes, so no schedule difference separates them from the main batch. Two of those three sessions were recovered from participant-supplied exports after an upload failure and verified against the partial rows already stored, agreeing on all 49 logged columns. Seven values in a column no analysis reads were set to the schedule's extinction sentinel.
