Study 2 · design proposal · 12 September 2026

Multiple control: which variable dominates, per individual

Half the session length, roughly a quarter of the cost, and about six times the transitions per operator estimate. The gain comes from not slicing the session into cells.

What study 1 established

Across 23 subjects, DMDc separated the organism's autonomous dynamics from environmental control, and the separation held. B flipped sign with the reversal for 22 of 23 subjects -- direct, per-subject evidence of stimulus control by color. The eigenvalues of A were stable across all four phases for 23 of 23, each subject's spread falling inside its own matched null, so A behaves as an individual invariant rather than a property of the condition in force. Counting control was rejected for 21 of 23: preference did not anticipate the cue.

What study 1 could not do is rank variables, because it contained only one. Color, contingency and response count were perfectly confounded — every block exactly 100 responses, zero jitter. Study 2 exists to break that confound and put several variables in competition.

0Why multiple control, and why now

Behavior rarely meets one stimulus at a time. When several are present, control divides among them, and how it divides has been studied since at least Ray's (1969) demonstration that combining stimuli which control incompatible behavior produces orderly intermediate performance. The question has stayed alive because the answer is not obvious: dimensions differ in salience, discriminability, and in how reliably they predict reinforcement, and these do not contribute equally.

The most directly relevant recent finding is Gomes-Ng, Elliffe, and Cowie (2026). Using matching-to-sample with compound stimuli in pigeons, they showed that divided control is graded by the global reinforcer probability associated with each dimension, and is largely insensitive to local, trial-by-trial contingencies signalling which dimension would pay next. They note this distinguishes divided stimulus control from concurrent choice, which tracks local reinforcer ratios closely (Davison & Baum, 2000, 2003; Cowie & Davison, 2016).

That contrast is the opening. Their measure was steady-state accuracy in a discrete-trial discrimination; the graded quantity was recovered by comparing performance across conditions. What has not been done is to measure divided control as a dynamical quantity in free-operant concurrent choice — where the local sensitivity they contrast against is precisely what is on display.

Study 1 of this program supplies the instrument. Fitting xt+1 = A xt + B ut + c per subject, with the signalling colour as u, the control term B reversed sign with the contingency reversal for 22 of 23 subjects, while the eigenvalues of A stayed inside each subject's own null for 23 of 23. So the decomposition separates what the organism brings from what the environment imposes, and the environmental half is a scalar with a sign that behaves as it should. With one cue, that is a manipulation check. With several, each column of B is the control exerted by one dimension, and their relative magnitudes are the division of control — measured per individual, from the time series, without a steady state.

One study 1 result bears directly on what to expect, and it has a direct precedent. Away from reversals, an autonomous model with ten bins of memory predicted the choice series as well as one given the cue (median gain −1.7%); the cue mattered only where its own history stopped predicting it. The organism's recent behavior was the better predictor of its next behavior.

Gomes-Ng, Austin, Bai, Landon, and Cowie (2026) report the same ordering in a different preparation. Pigeons in concurrent chains had terminal-link outcomes signalled both by the response that produced terminal-link entry — the organism's own recent behavior — and by keylight stimuli. When those sources conflicted, past behavior exerted strong control and overshadowed the signals, with some control by present stimuli emerging later in the terminal link. They conclude that egocentric stimuli can exert enduring control even when less transient discriminative stimuli were available in the recent past or present.

Pigeons in concurrent chains and humans in free-operant concurrent choice, one measured as terminal-link response allocation and the other as the variance an autonomous operator accounts for: different species, preparation and measure, same ordering. That makes a concrete prediction for study 2. A should dominate B in variance accounted for, and the question of interest is how the remainder divides among the cues. It also means the cues in study 2 compete not only with each other but with the subject's own history, which is precisely what the A/B split quantifies.

1The change that pays for everything

Study 1 spent 3,200 reversal responses on four color × contingency cells and fitted a separate operator in each. At a ten-response bin that left 36 transitions per estimate, which is why eigenvector similarity was uninterpretable: two estimates of the same operator at that count have a median similarity of 0.735 with a 5th–95th range of [0.087, 0.982].

If the cues vary continuously and independently instead of defining blocks, there are no cells. One operator per subject is fitted to the entire series, with the cues entering as columns of B.

2Design

2.1 Three cues, varying independently

Three binary stimulus dimensions, each switching on its own schedule with jittered intervals: background color (blue/green), panel texture (striped/dotted), and a tone played on each response (low/high). Each is present on every response; what varies is its value.

Each cue carries partial information about which key is currently rich, with different weights known by construction:

(2) logit P(left rich) = 1.6 ucolor + 0.8 utexture + 0.4 utone

So color is the dominant variable, texture half as informative, tone half again. The ground truth is known, which makes the central question falsifiable: does the fitted B recover the 4:2:1 ordering, per subject?

That is the whole point. Study 1 asked whether dynamics follow the contingency or the signal and could not answer it, partly for want of precision and partly because there was only one signal. Study 2 asks a question with a checkable answer.

Arranged weights are not discriminated weights. Gomes-Ng et al. (2026) make the point that stimulus control depends not only on the arranged correlation between stimulus, behavior and consequence but on the subject's ability to discriminate it, so that misallocation makes discriminated contingencies deviate from arranged ones and control comes out weaker than designed. The 4:2:1 in (2) is therefore a hypothesis about what is arranged, not a prediction that B will recover those ratios. A subject who cannot discriminate the tone at all should show a third column near zero, and that is a result about discriminability rather than a failure of the estimate.

2.2 Jitter, and why it is not optional

Every cue switches on a geometric interval with a mean of 60 responses and a minimum of 20, drawn independently per cue. Three consequences follow, each fixing a specific failure of study 1.

Count is decorrelated from cue. In study 1 every block was exactly 100 responses, so a subject could have been counting rather than attending. The anticipation test rejected that for 21 of 23, but the design should not have required a rescue test.

The cues become identifiable from each other. Independent switch schedules mean the three columns of u are close to orthogonal over 2,130 responses, which is what lets B's columns be estimated separately. With synchronized cues they would be collinear and no ranking would be possible.

The cue stops being predictable from its own past. In study 1 the cue was a zero-jitter square wave, self-predictable at 80–90%, so an autonomous model with ten bins of memory reproduced it and the cue added nothing measurable away from reversals. Under jitter the cue carries information no amount of history supplies.

2.3 Patch state carries across cue changes

Study 1 reset both alternatives to startingValue at every block, so a subject returned to a symmetric environment carrying no trace of what they had done to it. That is why the persistence question was unanswerable: there was nothing to persist. Here the patches are never reset; depletion and recovery run continuously for the whole session, and a cue change alters only which alternative recovers faster.

The probe block is cheap insurance. If B's columns come out collinear despite the jitter, the single-cue segments give an independent estimate of each dimension's control.

3What gets measured

Per subject, primary. The three columns of B, and their rank order against the designed 4:2:1. Reported as recovered weights with bootstrap intervals, per individual, not pooled.

Per subject, secondary. Eigenvalues of A at 212 transitions, which is where the same-operator floor becomes usable — it rises from 0.735 at n=36 to roughly 0.9 at n>200, with a far narrower interval. Study 1's central unanswerable question becomes answerable.

Design check. Correlations among the three cue series, which jitter should keep below about 0.2. If they are not, the ranking is not identified and the probe block is the fallback.

Study 1 cost $287.38 for 23 subjects of which three pilots bought the instrument. Study 2 at n=12 costs $53.28, a fifth of that, and gives each subject nearly six times the transitions.

The instrument already exists. The changes are: three cue dimensions instead of one, jittered switch schedules, patches that are not reset, and a shorter block plan. No change to logging, storage, export or the analysis pipeline beyond adding columns for the extra cues.

5Risks

Three cues may be too many for an 11-minute session. Subjects may not acquire control by the weakest cue at all, in which case its column of B is indistinguishable from zero. That is an interpretable outcome rather than a failure, but a two-cue version is the safer first run.

Continuous patch state may drift. Without resets, a subject who strongly prefers one alternative may drive it to the floor and stay there. The recovery rate needs re-tuning against a simulated responder before any money is spent, and the envelope check must cover the full range of response rates, as it now does.

An 11-minute task pays $3.33. Prolific's minimum is $8/hour; this is $18/hour. But short tasks attract faster, less careful responding, and study 1's own data show response rate varying three-fold across subjects.
